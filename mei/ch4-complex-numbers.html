Complex Numbers

Mathematics for Engineers I · Module 27651 · Chapter 4

This chapter extends the number line to a plane, so that equations such as z2 = −1 have solutions. You learn to calculate in normal and polar form, raise to powers and take roots, and meet the complex polynomial, exponential, logarithm, sine and cosine.

How the chapter fits together

A complex number is a point in a plane. Normal form describes it by its coordinates, polar form by its length and angle. Polar form turns multiplication into adding angles, which gives powers and roots; the exponential, logarithm, sine and cosine then extend to complex inputs through Euler’s formula.

4.1 Definitions and Basic Arithmetic Operations

No real number squares to −1, so an equation as simple as z2 + 1 = 0 has no real solution. Adding one new number and reading numbers as points of a plane fixes this, and every rule of real arithmetic still holds.

In plain words: picture a complex number as a point on a map, with east–west as one direction and north–south as the other. The new ingredient is the imaginary unit i, a number whose square is −1. Writing a number as “a plus i times b” is its normal form; a is its real part and b its imaginary part, and the map that holds these points is the Gaussian plane. You can also give a point by how far it is from the origin and in which direction: that is the polar form.

Normal form and the Gaussian plane

Normal formz = a + ibRe z = a, Im z = bi2 = −1Read it as: “z is a plus i b; a is the real part of z and b its imaginary part”.Here a, b ∈ ℝ, and the number is the ordered pair (a, b). A real number x is the complex number (x, 0). Two complex numbers are equal exactly when their real parts and their imaginary parts are equal. ℂ is a field, but it has no order: “z < w” means nothing.

Engineers often write j for the imaginary unit, because i (or I) already stands for current. The notes use i; in a circuit calculation, check which letter means what.

Calculating in normal form

Sum, difference and product(a1 + ib1) ± (a2 + ib2) = (a1 ± a2) + i(b1 ± b2)(a1 + ib1)(a2 + ib2) = (a1a2 − b1b2) + i(a1b2 + b1a2)Read it as: “add real parts to real parts and imaginary parts to imaginary parts”; for the product, “multiply out as with real numbers and replace i squared by −1”.Adding and subtracting work like vectors in the plane.
Quotientzw = (a1 + ib1)(a2 − ib2)(a2 + ib2)(a2 − ib2) = a1a2 + b1b2a22 + b22 − ia1b2 − b1a2a22 + b221a + ib = a − iba2 + b2Read it as: “multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the bottom”.Valid for w ≠ 0. The bottom becomes (a + ib)(a − ib) = a2 + b2, a real number. In particular 1i = −i.

Multiplying by i turns a point a quarter turn counterclockwise about the origin: i(a + ib) = −b + ia. Multiplying by i four times brings you back, since i4 = 1.

Worked example · the four operations

Let z = 3 + i and w = 1 − 2i.

  1. Sum and difference, part by part: z + w = 4 − i and z − w = 2 + 3i.
  2. Product: (3 + i)(1 − 2i) = 3 − 6i + i − 2i2 = 3 − 5i + 2 = 5 − 5i.
  3. Quotient: multiply top and bottom by 1 + 2i. The bottom is 12 + 22 = 5 and the top is (3 + i)(1 + 2i) = 3 + 6i + i + 2i2 = 1 + 7i, so zw = 15 + 75i.

Check the quotient by multiplying back: (1 − 2i)(1 + 7i) = 1 + 7i − 2i − 14i2 = 15 + 5i, and dividing by 5 gives 3 + i = z.

Conjugate, magnitude and distance

Conjugate and magnitudez̅ = a − ib|z| = √a2 + b2|z|2 = z · z̅Read it as: “z bar, the complex conjugate of z, is a minus i b”; “the magnitude of z is the square root of a squared plus b squared”; “z times its conjugate is the magnitude squared”.The conjugate reflects z in the real axis; |z| ≥ 0 is the distance of z from the origin. Hence 1z = z̅|z|2 for z ≠ 0.
Rules for conjugate and magnitudeconj(z + w) = z̅ + w̅conj(z · w) = z̅ · w̅Re z = z + z̅2Im z = z − z̅2i|zw| = |z| |w|||z| − |w|| ≤ |z + w| ≤ |z| + |w|Read it as: “the conjugate of a sum is the sum of the conjugates, and likewise for products”, where conj(z) means z̅; “the magnitude of a product is the product of the magnitudes”; the last line is the triangle inequality.z = z̅ exactly when z is real. These are the same rules the real absolute value obeys (Theorem 2.10).
Distance and circles|z − w| = √(a1 − a2)2 + (b1 − b2)2{z ∈ ℂ : |z − a| = r}Read it as: “the distance between z and w is the magnitude of their difference”, and “the set of z whose distance from a is r”.Here z = a1 + ib1, w = a2 + ib2. The second set is the circle with centre a and radius r; “< r” gives its inside and “> r” its outside.

Worked example · a disc in the plane

Describe the set M = {z ∈ ℂ : |z − (1 + i)| ≤ 2}, and decide whether 3 + i and 2 + 3i belong to it.

  1. |z − (1 + i)| is the distance from z to the point 1 + i, so M is the closed disc with centre 1 + i and radius 2, boundary included.
  2. For 3 + i: |(3 + i) − (1 + i)| = |2| = 2 ≤ 2, so it lies in M, on the boundary circle.
  3. For 2 + 3i: |1 + 2i| = √5 ≈ 2.24 > 2, so it lies outside M.

Check with a sketch: 3 + i is two units right of the centre, exactly on the circle, and 2 + 3i is further out, up and to the right.

Polar and exponential form

Polar form and Euler’s formulaz = |z|(cos φ + i sin φ)eiφ = cos φ + i sin φz = |z|eiφRead it as: “z is its magnitude times cos phi plus i sin phi”, and “e to the i phi is cos phi plus i sin phi” (Euler’s formula), so “z is its magnitude times e to the i phi”.φ = arg z is the argument, the angle from the positive real axis, taken in 0 ≤ φ < 2π. With r = |z|: a = r cos φ and b = r sin φ. Find φ from tan φ = ba using the quadrant of (a, b), exactly as for polar coordinates in §3.6. For z = 0 the angle is undefined.
Rules for eiφei(φ1 + φ2) = eiφ1eiφ2(eiφ)n = einφ|eiφ| = 1ei(φ + 2kπ) = eiφRead it as: “adding angles multiplies the exponentials”, and “e to the i phi always lies on the unit circle”.Here k ∈ ℤ. In the notes Euler’s formula is at first an abbreviation; it is proved properly later with Taylor series. It gives z̅ = |z|e−iφ, and z = w exactly when |z| = |w| and the arguments differ by a multiple of 2π.

Worked example · normal form to exponential form

Write z = −√3 + i in exponential form.

  1. The magnitude is |z| = √3 + 1 = √4 = 2.
  2. The real part is negative and the imaginary part positive, so z lies in quadrant II and the arctan value must be shifted by π.
  3. arctan(1−√3) = −π6, so φ = −π6 + π = 5π6.
  4. Therefore z = 2ei5π/6.

Check: 2 cos(5π6) = −√3 and 2 sin(5π6) = 1, which recovers −√3 + i.

Multiplying and dividing in polar form

Product and quotientzw = |z| |w| ei(φ + ψ)zw = |z||w| ei(φ − ψ)Read it as: “to multiply, multiply the lengths and add the angles; to divide, divide the lengths and subtract the angles”.Here z = |z|eiφ, w = |w|eiψ and w ≠ 0. This follows from the addition theorems for sine and cosine (§3.5). Geometrically, multiplying by w turns by ψ and stretches by |w|.

Worked example · multiply and divide in polar form

Let z = 2eiπ/3 and w = 4eiπ/6.

  1. Product: lengths 2 · 4 = 8, angles π3 + π6 = π2, so zw = 8eiπ/2 = 8i.
  2. Quotient: lengths 24 = 12, angles π3 − π6 = π6, so zw = 12eiπ/6 = 12(√32 + 12i) = √34 + 14i.

Check the product in normal form: z = 1 + i√3 and w = 2√3 + 2i, and (1 + i√3)(2√3 + 2i) = 2√3 + 2i + 6i − 2√3 = 8i.

Trap: |z|2 is not z2 for complex numbers. For z = 1 + i, |z|2 = (1 + i)(1 − i) = 2, but (1 + i)2 = 2i. Only for real numbers do the two agree.

Sign check: arctan(ba) only returns angles between −π2 and π2. Decide the quadrant from the signs of a and b first, then add π (quadrants II and III) or 2π (quadrant IV) to land in [0, 2π). On the imaginary axis (a = 0) the quotient is undefined: φ = π2 for b > 0 and φ = 3π2 for b < 0.

Memory hook: normal form for adding, polar form for multiplying. Each form makes one operation easy and the other awkward.

Near miss: for z = −1 − i, tan φ = −1−1 = 1 suggests φ = π4, but that angle points into quadrant I. Both parts are negative, so add π: φ = 5π4 and z = √2ei5π/4.

4.2 Roots and Powers

Polar multiplication from §4.1 turns a power into one stretch and one turn. Run backwards, it solves wn = z, which always has exactly n answers in ℂ.

In plain words: raising a number to the power k stretches its length to the k-th power and turns its angle k times over; this is de Moivre’s formula. An n-th root undoes that: take the ordinary root of the length and divide the angle by n. Because an angle is only known up to whole turns, dividing gives n different answers, spread evenly round a circle like the corners of a regular polygon.

Powers

De Moivre’s formulazk = |z|k(cos(kφ) + i sin(kφ)) = |z|keikφRead it as: “z to the k is the magnitude to the k times e to the i k phi”.Here z = |z|eiφ and k ∈ ℕ. It is polar multiplication of z by itself k times. Reduce kφ by multiples of 2π to bring it back into [0, 2π).

Worked example · a sixth power

Compute (1 − i)6.

  1. Polar form: |1 − i| = √2, and the point is in quadrant IV, so φ = 7π4 and 1 − i = √2ei7π/4.
  2. De Moivre: the length becomes (√2)6 = 8 and the angle 6 · 7π4 = 21π2.
  3. Reduce the angle: 21π2 = 10π + π2, so the angle is π2 and (1 − i)6 = 8eiπ/2 = 8i.

Check by squaring in normal form: (1 − i)2 = −2i, and (−2i)3 = −8i3 = 8i.

n-th roots

The n roots of a complex numberwn = zwk = |z|1/n ei(φ + 2kπ)/n, k = 0, 1, …, n − 1Read it as: “the k-th root w k is the real n-th root of the magnitude times e to the i, phi plus 2 k pi, over n”.Here z = |z|eiφ ≠ 0 and n ∈ ℕ; |z|1/n is the ordinary non-negative real root. There are exactly n distinct roots. They are the corners of a regular n-gon on the circle of radius |z|1/n, neighbours 2πn apart, the first at angle φn.

Why the formula: write w = |w|eiψ. Then wn = |w|neinψ, and this equals |z|eiφ exactly when

  1. |w|n = |z|, so |w| = |z|1/n, and
  2. nψ = φ + 2kπ for some k ∈ ℤ, so ψ = φ + 2kπn.
  3. k = n gives the angle of k = 0 plus a full turn, so only k = 0, …, n − 1 give different numbers.
Rules for rootsz1/n · w1/n = (z · w)1/nz1/nw1/n = (zw)1/n(z1/n)1/m = z1/(nm)Read it as: “the product of n-th roots is an n-th root of the product”, and “an m-th root of an n-th root is an (n m)-th root”.For w ≠ 0 in the quotient and m, n ∈ ℕ. Because each root has n values, these hold only for suitably chosen values, not for any choice.

Worked example · the cube roots of −8

Solve w3 = −8.

  1. Polar form of the right side: −8 = 8eiπ, so |z| = 8 and φ = π.
  2. Length of every root: 81/3 = 2.
  3. Angles: π + 2kπ3 for k = 0, 1, 2, that is π3, π and 5π3.
  4. The roots are w0 = 2eiπ/3 = 1 + i√3, w1 = 2eiπ = −2 and w2 = 2ei5π/3 = 1 − i√3.

Check: (−2)3 = −8, and by de Moivre (2eiπ/3)3 = 8eiπ = −8. The three roots also sum to 0, as the corners of an equilateral triangle centred at the origin must.

Application: phasors in AC circuits

An alternating voltage U(t) = U0 sin(ωt) is replaced by the rotating complex voltage U(t) = U0eiωt; the real signal is recovered as its imaginary part. A phase shift then becomes a fixed angle between rotating arrows, and derivatives become multiplication by iω.

Current through a coilUL = ddt II = UiωL = U0ωL ei(ωt − π/2)Read it as: “the voltage over L is the rate of change of current”, so “the current phasor is the voltage phasor divided by i omega L”.L in H, ω in s−1, U0 in V, so I0 = U0ωL is in A. Since 1i = −i = e−iπ/2, the current lags the voltage by π2. (The notes write Û and Î for the amplitudes U0, I0, and w for ω.)

Worked example · amplitude and phase of a coil current

A coil with L = 0.2 H is driven by U(t) = 10 V · sin(50 s−1 · t).

  1. Amplitude: I0 = U0ωL = 10 V50 s−1 · 0.2 H = 1 A.
  2. Phase: the factor 1i = e−iπ/2 shifts the current by −π2, so I(t) = 1 A · sin(50 s−1 · t − π2).

Check the units: V divided by s−1 · H is V divided by Ω, which is A, since 1 H · s−1 = 1 Ω.

Trap: the n-th root of a complex number is not one number. Writing “√−1 = i” picks one of two square roots of −1; the other is −i.

Sign check: multiplying by i turns counterclockwise by π2; dividing by i turns clockwise by π2. That is why a coil’s current lags its voltage.

Memory hook: for n ≥ 2 the n roots balance around the origin, so they add up to 0. If yours do not, an angle is wrong.

Near miss: “√z · √w = √zw” with z = w = −1 and the value i for each root gives i · i = −1, but √1 = 1 for the value 1. The rule needs suitably chosen values: taking −1 as the value of √1 makes it hold.

4.3 Special Complex Functions

With complex numbers available, polynomials always split into linear factors, and the exponential, logarithm, sine and cosine extend to complex inputs. The extensions keep the familiar rules but gain new behaviour: the exponential repeats, and the logarithm has to choose an angle.

In plain words: over the complex numbers, a polynomial of degree n always has n zeros, counted with repeats; this is the fundamental theorem of algebra. The complex exponential e to the x + iy stretches by e to the x and turns by y; since a full turn changes nothing, it repeats when you add 2πi. To undo it, the complex logarithm must pick one angle from each family, and the notes take the angle in [0, 2π). Complex sine and cosine are built from exponentials.

Polynomials

Fundamental theorem of algebraP(z) = anzn + … + a1z + a0P(z) = an(z − z1)(z − z2) … (z − zn)Read it as: “a complex polynomial of degree n factors completely into n linear factors”.Coefficients a0, …, an ∈ ℂ with an ≠ 0, and n ∈ ℕ. The zeros z1, …, zn need not be distinct; grouping equal ones gives an(z − z1)m1 … (z − zl)ml, where the multiplicities add up to n. The notes state this without proof.
Real coefficientsP(z0) = 0 ⇒ P(z̅0) = 0(z − z0)(z − z̅0) = z2 − 2 Re(z0) z + |z0|2Read it as: “if z nought is a zero, so is its conjugate”, and “the pair multiplies to a quadratic with real coefficients”.Holds when every coefficient is real. Consequences: non-real zeros come in conjugate pairs; a polynomial of odd degree has at least one real zero; and the polynomial factors into real linear and real quadratic factors.

Worked example · factor over ℂ and over ℝ

Find all zeros of P(z) = z3 − z2 + 4z − 4 and factor it both ways.

  1. Group the terms: z2(z − 1) + 4(z − 1) = (z − 1)(z2 + 4).
  2. z2 + 4 = 0 gives z2 = −4 = 4eiπ, whose two square roots are 2eiπ/2 = 2i and 2ei3π/2 = −2i.
  3. Over ℂ: P(z) = (z − 1)(z − 2i)(z + 2i). Over ℝ: P(z) = (z − 1)(z2 + 4).

Check with the pair formula: for z0 = 2i, −2 Re(2i) = 0 and |2i|2 = 4, giving z2 + 4. Degree 3 and three zeros, one of them real, as odd degree requires.

The complex exponential

Complex exponential functionez = ex + iy = ex(cos y + i sin y)|ez| = exez + 2kπi = ezez + w = ezewRead it as: “e to the x plus i y is e to the x times cos y plus i sin y”: the real part sets the length, the imaginary part the angle.Defined for every z ∈ ℂ. Unlike the real exponential it is periodic, with period 2πi (k ∈ ℤ). It is never 0, because its length ex is positive.

Worked example · evaluate an exponential

Compute e2 + iπ/3 in normal form and give its magnitude.

  1. Split the exponent: real part 2, imaginary part π3.
  2. e2 + iπ/3 = e2(cos π3 + i sin π3) = e2(12 + i√32).
  3. With e2 ≈ 7.389: e2 + iπ/3 ≈ 3.69 + 6.40i, and |e2 + iπ/3| = e2 ≈ 7.39.

Check: √3.692 + 6.402 ≈ √54.6 ≈ 7.39, the same length.

The complex logarithm

Complex logarithmln z = ln|z| + iφeln z = zRead it as: “ln z is the real log of the magnitude plus i times the angle”.For z = |z|eiφ ≠ 0 with 0 ≤ φ < 2π, so the domain is ℂ\{0}, and every value lies in the strip 0 ≤ Im < 2π. Without fixing the angle range, ln z would be defined only up to adding 2kπi. Negative real numbers now have logarithms: ln(−1) = iπ.

Worked example · two logarithms

Find ln(−e) and ln(1 + i).

  1. −e = e · eiπ, so ln(−e) = ln e + iπ = 1 + iπ.
  2. 1 + i = √2eiπ/4, so ln(1 + i) = ln √2 + iπ4 = 12 ln 2 + iπ4 ≈ 0.347 + 0.785i.

Check the second: eln(1 + i) = e(ln 2)/2eiπ/4 = √2(√22 + i√22) = 1 + i.

Complex sine and cosine

Sine and cosine of a complex numbersin z = eiz − e−iz2icos z = eiz + e−iz2Read it as: “sine z is e to the i z minus e to the minus i z, over 2 i”, and likewise for cosine with a plus over 2.For real x these follow from adding and subtracting eix = cos x + i sin x and e−ix = cos x − i sin x; for complex z they are the definition. They keep period 2π, sin2 z + cos2 z = 1 and the addition theorems, and their zeros are only the real ones: sin z = 0 ⇔ z = kπ, cos z = 0 ⇔ z = kπ + π2, k ∈ ℤ.

Worked example · cosine of i

Compute cos(i) and sin(i), and check the circle equation.

  1. With z = i: iz = −1, so cos(i) = e−1 + e12 ≈ 0.368 + 2.7182 ≈ 1.543.
  2. sin(i) = e−1 − e12i = i · e − e−12 ≈ 1.175i.
  3. sin2(i) + cos2(i) ≈ −1.381 + 2.381 = 1.

Check: the circle equation holds. Note that cos(i) > 1: in ℂ, sine and cosine are not bounded by 1. (This follows directly from the definition; the notes do not state it.)

Trap: the rule ln(zw) = ln z + ln w can fail in ℂ, because adding two angles can leave [0, 2π). For z = w = −1: ln(1) = 0, but ln(−1) + ln(−1) = 2πi. (This follows directly from the definition; the notes only define ln z.)

Sign check: with the notes’ convention the angle of ln z is never negative: ln(−i) = i3π2, not −iπ2.

Memory hook: real coefficients mean a mirror-symmetric picture of zeros. A zero above the real axis always has its twin below.

Near miss: z2 + iz + 2 has the zero i (check: −1 − 1 + 2 = 0), but −i is not a zero (−1 + 1 + 2 = 2). The conjugate-pair rule fails here because the coefficient i is not real.

Formula map and checks

Complex numbers carry no units in this chapter except in the phasor application, where voltages are in V, currents in A, inductances in H and ω in s−1. Angles are always in radians.

Explain it

Answer aloud in complete sentences. Then compare with the model answer. (Retrieval and explanatory questioning [MIS, AMN, NEW].)

  1. Why does dividing by a complex number start by multiplying by the conjugate of the denominator?
  2. Why is “z < w” meaningless for complex numbers?
  3. When is polar form better than normal form, and when is it worse?
  4. Why does the equation wn = z have n solutions and not just one?
  5. Why do the non-real zeros of a real polynomial come in pairs?
  6. Why does the complex logarithm need a fixed angle range?
  7. When does a phasor calculation turn a derivative into a multiplication?
  8. Why is |z|2 = zz̅ more useful than |z|2 = z2?
Show model answers
  1. Because (a + ib)(a − ib) = a2 + b2 is real. Multiplying top and bottom by the same number leaves the quotient unchanged, and a real denominator can be divided into each part.
  2. An order would have to be compatible with multiplication, so squares would be non-negative. But i2 = −1 is a negative square, so no such order exists on ℂ.
  3. Polar form is better for multiplication, division, powers and roots, because lengths multiply and angles add. It is worse for addition and subtraction, which are simple only in normal form.
  4. An angle is fixed only up to whole turns. Dividing φ + 2kπ by n gives n different angles before they start to repeat, and each gives a different root.
  5. Conjugation does not change real coefficients, so conjugating P(z0) = 0 gives P(z̅0) = 0. A non-real zero and its conjugate are different numbers, so they form a pair.
  6. ez repeats every 2πi, so infinitely many numbers have the same exponential. To get a function, one angle must be chosen, and the notes take 0 ≤ φ < 2π.
  7. When the signal is written as U0eiωt: differentiating in time multiplies it by iω, so a differential relation like the coil’s becomes an algebraic one.
  8. zz̅ is always a non-negative real number, as a squared length must be. z2 is in general complex, for example (1 + i)2 = 2i.

Exam-style problems

Allow about 20 minutes for each. Work every part before checking the answer. Afterward, note where you lost a step and revisit the cited sections.

1 · Roots and a real polynomial

Consider P(z) = z3 − 8.

  1. Write 8 in exponential form and find all three solutions of z3 = 8 in normal form.
  2. Show that the two non-real solutions are conjugates, and say why this had to happen.
  3. Factor P into a real linear and a real quadratic factor.
  4. Use polar multiplication to find the product of the three solutions, and check it against the factorisation.
Show the worked answer
  1. 8 = 8ei0. The roots have length 81/3 = 2 and angles 0, 2π3, 4π3: z0 = 2, z1 = 2ei2π/3 = −1 + i√3, z2 = 2ei4π/3 = −1 − i√3. §4.2
  2. −1 + i√3 and −1 − i√3 differ only in the sign of the imaginary part. P has real coefficients, so the conjugate of a zero is a zero. §4.3
  3. The pair gives z2 − 2 Re(−1 + i√3)z + |−1 + i√3|2 = z2 + 2z + 4, so P(z) = (z − 2)(z2 + 2z + 4). Multiplying out gives z3 − 8. §4.3
  4. Lengths 2 · 2 · 2 = 8 and angles 0 + 2π3 + 4π3 = 2π, so the product is 8ei2π = 8. In (z − z0)(z − z1)(z − z2) the constant term is −z0z1z2 = −8, which matches. §4.1

2 · Powers, logarithms and the angle range

Let z = 1 − i.

  1. Write z in exponential form with 0 ≤ φ < 2π.
  2. Compute z5 in normal form and check it by multiplying in normal form.
  3. Compute ln z.
  4. Compute ln(z2) and 2 ln z, and explain why they differ.
Show the worked answer
  1. |z| = √2; the point is in quadrant IV, so φ = −π4 + 2π = 7π4 and z = √2ei7π/4. §4.1
  2. Length (√2)5 = 4√2, angle 35π4 = 8π + 3π4, so z5 = 4√2ei3π/4 = 4√2(−√22 + i√22) = −4 + 4i. Check: (1 − i)2 = −2i, (1 − i)4 = (−2i)2 = −4, and −4(1 − i) = −4 + 4i. §4.2
  3. ln z = ln √2 + i7π4 ≈ 0.347 + 5.50i. §4.3
  4. z2 = −2i = 2ei3π/2, so ln(z2) = ln 2 + i3π2 ≈ 0.693 + 4.71i. But 2 ln z = ln 2 + i7π2 ≈ 0.693 + 11.0i. The angle 7π2 lies outside [0, 2π); the two differ by 2πi, a full turn the logarithm’s angle range removes. §4.3 §4.1

Slips in the notes

These are source-checked issues. Printed page numbers refer to the lecture notes.

  1. Printed page 42, powers of 1 + i: the closed form reads as the k-th root of 2 times eikπ/4. For the list that follows to be right it must be (√2)k = 2k/2, which is what the computed values z1, …, z8 use (for example z8 = 16).
  2. Printed page 46, multiplicities: the remark allows multiplicities mj ∈ ℕ0. A number that actually is a zero has multiplicity at least 1, so ℕ is meant.
  3. Printed pages 44–45, notation: the phasor application writes the angular frequency as w; this guide writes ω. Not an error, but easy to confuse with a complex number w.
  4. Printed page 36, notation: the notes introduce i := √−1. By Theorem 4.9 the square root of −1 has two values, i and −i; the definition means the number with i2 = −1, which is how this guide states it.
