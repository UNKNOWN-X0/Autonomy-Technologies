DC Networks

Electrical Engineering I · Module 92315 · Chapter 3

Chapters 1 and 2 described fields inside components; this chapter treats each component as a box with two terminals and a current–voltage rule. With sources, Kirchhoff’s two laws and a few reduction rules you can find every current and voltage in a resistor network, and judge how much power reaches a load.

How the chapter fits together

Every component is reduced to its terminal rule: a resistor obeys Ohm’s law, a source fixes a voltage or a current. Kirchhoff’s two laws then tie the components together. Everything else in the chapter is a shortcut built from those three ingredients, or, for larger networks, a systematic way to write them down.

3.1 Introduction

Chapters 1 and 2 computed resistances and capacitances from field pictures. From now on those values are given, and the question becomes how connected components share voltages and currents.

In plain words: a real component is replaced by an idealised one that behaves the same at its terminals: a metal cylinder becomes “a resistor of 47 Ω”. The manufacturer does the field modelling and prints the result in a data sheet. Connecting such idealised parts gives an electrical network, and network calculation finds all its currents and voltages. The chapter uses constant (DC) values, but every method carries over to alternating currents in Chapter 6.

ConductanceG = 1R[G] = S = Ω−1Read it as: “G is one over R, measured in siemens”.Every resistance in this chapter is positive, so every resistor also has a conductance. Conductance is the natural quantity for parallel connections (§3.5) and node equations (§3.6).
Field quantity (Chapters 1–2)Lumped valueTerminal rule used here
Conductor of given shape and conductivityResistance RU = RI

Worked example · conductance and current

A data sheet lists a resistor as 2.5 kΩ. It sits across a constant 10 V.

  1. Conductance: G = 12500 Ω = 0.4 mS.
  2. Current from Ohm’s law: I = GU = 0.4 mS · 10 V = 4 mA.
  3. The same from the resistance: I = 10 V2.5 kΩ = 4 mA.

Check the units: S · V = A, and V ÷ Ω = A. A larger resistance gives a smaller conductance and a smaller current, as it should.

Trap: the lumped value only describes behaviour at the terminals. Two resistors of 100 Ω with very different shapes are the same part to the network.

Memory hook: resistance adds in series, conductance adds in parallel. Keep both in your head from the start.

Near miss: an ideal connecting wire has zero resistance, so it has no finite conductance, and “G = 1R” fails for it. That is why the notes assume R > 0 for every resistor and treat wires separately as perfect conductors.

3.2 Voltage and Current Sources

Every network needs something that drives it. §3.1 described the passive parts; sources are the active ones, and how you model them decides which currents are even possible.

In plain words: an ideal voltage source holds its voltage whatever you connect, like a perfectly stiff pump pressure; an ideal current source pushes the same current whatever you connect. Real batteries and lab supplies sag under load, so a real source is modelled as an ideal one plus an internal resistance. The two real models can be swapped without the outside noticing (source transformation). A dependent source has a value set by a voltage or current elsewhere in the network.

Ideal sources

Ideal voltage source and ideal current sourceU = U0, I set by the loadI = I0, U set by the loadRead it as: “the voltage source fixes U and the load decides I; the current source fixes I and the load decides U”.With a resistor R as load: I = U0R for the voltage source, U = RI0 for the current source. Two forbidden cases: short-circuiting an ideal voltage source (the current would grow without limit) and leaving an ideal current source open (the voltage would grow without limit). Circuit symbols: the standards’ general symbols are a circle with a line along the wire (voltage source) or across it (current source). The notes prefer telling symbols: a circle with “=” for DC, with “~” for AC, with an arrow for an arbitrary waveform; in their circuit figures, and in this guide’s, a current source is drawn as a circle with an arrow.

Real sources

Real voltage sourceU = U0 − IRiI0 = U0RiRead it as: “the terminal voltage is U nought minus the drop I times R i”.I0 is the short-circuit current, U0 the open-circuit voltage (no load, I = 0) and Ri the internal resistance in series. Any two of U0, Ri and I0 fix the source. Units: V, Ω, A.
Real current sourceI = I0 − URiU0 = I0RiRead it as: “the delivered current is I nought minus what the internal resistance takes”.U0 is the open-circuit voltage. Here Ri sits in parallel with the ideal current source and takes the part Ii = URi of I0. I0 is now the short-circuit current (U = 0).

Source transformation

Equivalent real sourcesU0 = I0Risame Ri in both modelsRead it as: “the voltage source equals the current source when U nought is I nought times R i”.A real voltage source (U0 in series with Ri) and a real current source (I0 in parallel with the same Ri) give the same terminal line U = U0 − IRi, so any load sees identical U and I. Inside they differ: with no load the voltage source wastes nothing, while the current source turns U0I0 into heat in Ri; with a short circuit it is the other way round.

Derivation: why the two models agree

  1. Voltage model, KVL round source, Ri and terminals: U = U0 − IRi.
  2. Current model, KCL at the top node: I0 splits into Ii = URi through Ri and I to the load, so I = I0 − URi.
  3. Multiply the second line by Ri: U = I0Ri − IRi.
  4. This is the first line exactly when U0 = I0Ri: both models then share one terminal line.

Worked example · one battery, two models

A battery has U0 = 12 V and Ri = 0.5 Ω and feeds a load R = 5.5 Ω.

  1. Voltage-source model: I = 12 V0.5 Ω + 5.5 Ω = 2 A and U = 12 V − 2 A · 0.5 Ω = 11 V.
  2. Short-circuit current: I0 = 12 V0.5 Ω = 24 A, so the current-source model is 24 A in parallel with 0.5 Ω.
  3. Current-source model with the same load: the current divider (§3.5) sends I = 24 A · 0.5 Ω0.5 Ω + 5.5 Ω = 2 A into the load, so U = 2 A · 5.5 Ω = 11 V.
  4. Open terminals: the voltage-source model dissipates nothing, the current-source model 24 A · 12 V = 288 W in its Ri.

Check: both models give 2 A and 11 V at the load, and 11 V = 2 A · 5.5 Ω. Only the internal losses differ, which is the point of the “keep in mind” remark in the notes.

Dependent sources are ideal and do not feed back on the controlling quantity, which can sit anywhere in the network. They use the same symbols as independent sources, with a function in place of a fixed value, and they model transistors and operational amplifiers in equivalent circuits. (The units of the factors follow directly from the output and control quantities; the notes do not list them.)

Worked example · a voltage-controlled current source

A model of an amplifier stage has a voltage-controlled current source with β = 20 mS, controlled by an input voltage Ucg = 0.5 V. Its output current flows through a 1 kΩ resistor.

  1. Output current: Icd = βUcg = 20 mS · 0.5 V = 10 mA.
  2. Output voltage across the resistor: 10 mA · 1 kΩ = 10 V.
  3. Voltage gain: 10 V0.5 V = 20.

Check the units: S · V = A and A · Ω = V. Doubling the input doubles the output, because the source is linear.

Trap: I0 means different things: in a real current source it is the ideal source’s current, in a real voltage source it is the short-circuit current U0Ri. After a transformation the two coincide, which is why the notes reuse the symbol.

Sign check: the terminal voltage of a real voltage source is never above U0 while it delivers current: U = U0 − IRi with I ≥ 0.

Near miss: a 9 V source in parallel with a 3 Ω resistor looks like the transformation pattern, but it is not a real current source: an ideal voltage source in parallel with anything still fixes 9 V at the terminals, so the resistor changes nothing outside. The transformation needs Ri in series with a voltage source or in parallel with a current source.

3.3 Definitions, Network Graph

Before any law can be written down, every voltage and current needs a name and a direction. This section fixes the vocabulary and the sign conventions that §3.4 to §3.6 rely on.

In plain words: passive components such as resistors use or store energy; active components such as sources feed it in. You reach a component only through its terminals. A pair of terminals is a port when the current going in at one equals the current coming out at the other. Strip a circuit down to its skeleton and you get a network graph: branches (the parts, as lines) meeting at nodes (dots), and closed paths through branches called meshes. Each branch voltage and current gets a reference arrow, the direction in which it counts as positive.

Terminals, ports and graphs

Port conditionI1′ = I1Read it as: “the current leaving terminal 1 prime equals the current entering terminal 1”.A two-terminal part is always a port, because what flows in must flow out (charge conservation from Chapter 2). A four-terminal part is a two-port when both terminal pairs meet the condition; once one pair does, the other does too.
TermMeaningHow to count it
Branch (b)A two-terminal part of the network, one or more componentsOne line in the graph. A bare connecting wire is not a branch.
Node (n)A terminal of a branch or a junction of several branches; it has one potentialPoints joined only by wire are one node.
Mesh (m)A closed sequence of branches through nodesWrite it as a list of branches, with a minus sign for a branch passed against its arrow.

Reference arrows and sign conventions

Reference directionsU12 = φe(P1) − φe(P2)Read it as: “U one two is the potential at P one minus the potential at P two”.The voltage arrow points along the integration path of E, from P1 to P2; a current arrow points along the area element dA used to compute it. Currents and voltages stay scalars: the arrow only tells you how to read the sign. A negative value means the actual direction is against the arrow.
Load and generator conventionP = UIRead it as: “load: same direction, the part takes power; generator: opposite directions, the part gives power”.Load convention: the U and I arrows point the same way through the part. Generator convention: they point opposite ways. Use the load convention for passive parts (then U = RI with a plus sign) and the generator convention for sources. With the load convention P > 0 means the part absorbs power; with the generator convention P > 0 means it delivers power. (The power reading follows directly from P = UI in Chapter 2; the notes describe it in words.) Fix one arrow freely; the convention then fixes the other.

Worked example · counting and signs

An ideal source U0 sits between nodes n0 and n1; R1 joins n1 to n2; R2 and R3 both join n2 to n0.

  1. Nodes: n0, n1, n2, so three. Wire segments along the bottom all belong to n0.
  2. Branches: the source, R1, R2 and R3, so four, as in the figure.
  3. Meshes: for example (b1, b2, b3) round the left, and (b3, −b4) through the two parallel resistors.
  4. A part measured with the load convention shows U = 5 V and I = −2 A. Then P = −10 W: it delivers 10 W, so it acts as a source.

Check: a mesh must end at the node where it started; (b3, −b4) goes n2 → n0 along b3 and back n0 → n2 against b4’s arrow.

Trap: two dots joined only by a wire are one node, even if the drawing shows two. Count potentials, not dots.

Memory hook: load: arrows parallel, power in. Generator: arrows opposite, power out.

Near miss: a four-terminal box with 4 mA in at terminal 1 and 3 mA out at terminal 1′ is not a two-port: the missing 1 mA leaves through the other pair, so neither pair meets the port condition.

3.4 Kirchhoff’s Circuit Laws

Ohm’s law describes each part on its own; it says nothing about how parts are connected. Kirchhoff’s two laws supply exactly the missing equations, and they come straight from the field laws of Chapters 1 and 2.

In plain words: walk round any closed loop of a circuit and add the potential steps: you end at the height you started from, so the steps add to zero. That is Kirchhoff’s voltage law (KVL). And at any junction, what flows in must flow out, because charge cannot pile up in a stationary current: Kirchhoff’s current law (KCL). Together with each part’s current–voltage rule, these give enough equations to find every unknown.

Kirchhoff’s voltage law

KVL∑μ ∈ mesh Uμ = 0Read it as: “the sum of the branch voltages round a mesh is zero”.Go round the mesh in either direction. A voltage whose arrow points along the direction of travel counts +, one against it counts −. The mesh need not follow wires: a voltage between two unconnected points (such as an unknown Ux) can close it.

Derivation: KVL from the field

  1. The electrostatic field has no circulation: ∮ E · ds = 0 round any closed path (Chapter 1).
  2. Split the path at the nodes P1, P2, …, Pk it passes. Each piece is a potential difference, for example φP1 − φP2, which is the branch voltage between those nodes, with a minus sign when its arrow points against the travel.
  3. The sum of the pieces telescopes, (φP1 − φP2) + (φP2 − φP3) + … + (φPk − φP1) = 0, which is ∑ Uμ = 0.

Worked example · an unknown voltage in a mesh

In the figure U0 = 12 V, U1 = 3 V and U2 = 5 V. Find U3.

  1. Travel clockwise. Against the travel: U0. Along it: U1, U2, U3.
  2. KVL: −U0 + U1 + U2 + U3 = 0.
  3. U3 = 12 V − 3 V − 5 V = 4 V.

Check: the source’s 12 V is shared out among the three resistors, 3 + 5 + 4 = 12. A negative result would have meant U3 points the other way.

Kirchhoff’s current law

KCL∑μ ∈ node Iμ = 0Read it as: “the sum of the currents at a node is zero”.Count a current + when its arrow points into the node and − when it points out (or the other way round, consistently). The node may be a closed surface round a whole region of the circuit: the currents crossing the surface still add to zero. Of the equations for all N nodes, one is always redundant.

Derivation: KCL from charge conservation

  1. For stationary currents the charge inside any closed surface stays constant, so ∯ J · dA = 0 (Chapter 2).
  2. Put the surface round a node. It cuts only the wires that meet there, and the flux through each wire’s cross-section is that wire’s current, + for an inward arrow and − for an outward one.
  3. The flux integral becomes ∑ Iμ = 0: inflow equals outflow.

Worked example · currents at a node and round a region

At a node, 3 A flows in on wire 1, 1.2 A flows out on wire 2, and wire 3 carries −0.8 A with its arrow pointing in. Wire 4’s arrow points out.

  1. KCL with inward arrows +: 3 A − 1.2 A + (−0.8 A) − I4 = 0.
  2. I4 = 1.0 A, flowing out as its arrow says.
  3. Wire 3’s negative value means 0.8 A actually leaves the node; KCL handles that without redrawing the arrow.

Check by actual directions: in 3 A; out 1.2 + 0.8 + 1.0 = 3 A.

Trap: sign errors come from the arrows, not the values. Decide the travel direction (KVL) or the in/out rule (KCL) first, write the signs, and only then insert numbers.

Sign check: an ideal current source in a mesh still has a voltage; it is just unknown. KVL is how you find it.

Near miss: a 2 A current source drives a loop through a 5 Ω resistor and a 4 V ideal voltage source whose + terminal the current enters. Setting the current source’s voltage to zero breaks KVL (10 V + 4 V ≠ 0). Its voltage is the unknown that closes the loop: 14 V.

3.5 Simple Resistor Networks

With Kirchhoff’s laws (§3.4) every network can be solved, but most of the time a few parts can be merged first. Series and parallel rules, dividers and the source–load relations are the shortcuts you will use in almost every problem.

In plain words: parts in series carry the same current, one after another like beads on a string; parts in parallel share the same voltage, side by side like lanes of a road. A voltage divider uses a series chain to tap off part of a voltage; a current divider uses parallel branches to split a current. Attaching something to a divider loads it and changes the split. Finally, a real source delivers the most power when the load matches its internal resistance (maximum power transfer), but then half the power is lost inside, so the efficiency is only 50 %.

Series and parallel

Series connectionRtotal = ∑n=1N RnRead it as: “in series the resistances add”.Same current through every resistor; the voltages add (KVL). Rtotal is larger than the largest single resistance.
Parallel connectionGtotal = ∑n=1N GnR1 ∥ R2 = 11R1 + 1R2 = R1R2R1 + R2Read it as: “in parallel the conductances add; two resistors give product over sum”.Same voltage across every resistor; the currents add (KCL). Rtotal is smaller than the smallest single resistance; N equal resistors R in parallel give RN. The symbol ∥ is the parallel operator; the product-over-sum form only works for two resistors at a time. These rules are dual to those for capacitors, which add in parallel.

Derivation: series and parallel

  1. Series: one current I flows through every resistor, and KVL adds the voltages: Utotal = ∑ RnI. Dividing by I gives Rtotal = ∑ Rn.
  2. Parallel: one voltage U lies across every resistor, and KCL adds the currents: Itotal = ∑ URn = U ∑ Gn. Dividing by U gives Gtotal = ∑ Gn.
  3. Two resistors: 11R1 + 1R2 = R1R2R2 + R1, multiplying top and bottom by R1R2.

Worked example · reduce a network step by step

A 6 Ω resistor is in series with a parallel pair of 12 Ω and 4 Ω, and the chain is connected to 18 V.

  1. Parallel pair: 12 Ω ∥ 4 Ω = 12 · 412 + 4 Ω = 3 Ω.
  2. Series with 6 Ω: Rtotal = 9 Ω, so the current from the source is 18 V9 Ω = 2 A.
  3. Voltage across the pair: 2 A · 3 Ω = 6 V; across the 6 Ω resistor: 12 V.
  4. Branch currents: 6 V12 Ω = 0.5 A and 6 V4 Ω = 1.5 A.

Check: 0.5 A + 1.5 A = 2 A (KCL) and 12 V + 6 V = 18 V (KVL). The 3 Ω result is below the smaller resistor, 4 Ω, as a parallel result must be.

Voltage divider

Unloaded voltage dividerUnUtotal = RnRtotalUkUn = RkRntwo resistors: U2 = R2R1 + R2 URead it as: “each part of the voltage is in the same ratio to the total as its resistance is to the total”.Only for a series chain, where one current flows through every resistor: each resistor takes a share of the voltage in proportion to its resistance.
Loaded voltage dividerRpar = R2 ∥ RL = R2RLR2 + RLU2U = RparR1 + Rpar = R2RLR1R2 + RL(R1 + R2)Read it as: “U two over U is R par over R one plus R par”.A load RL at the tap draws IL, so R2 no longer carries the full current and the unloaded ratio fails. Combine R2 with RL first; the result is again an unloaded divider. For RL → ∞ it returns to R2R1 + R2. Keep RL large compared with R2 if the tap voltage must not sag.

Derivation: unloaded and loaded divider

  1. Unloaded: one current I = UR1 + R2 flows through both, so U2 = R2I = R2R1 + R2 U.
  2. Loaded: RL is in parallel with R2, so replace R2 by Rpar = R2RLR2 + RL and use the unloaded rule: U2U = RparR1 + Rpar.
  3. Multiply top and bottom by (R2 + RL): U2U = R2RLR1(R2 + RL) + R2RL = R2RLR1R2 + RL(R1 + R2).
  4. For RL → ∞ divide top and bottom by RL: the ratio tends to R2R1 + R2, the unloaded value.

Worked example · how much a load pulls down a divider

U = 12 V and R1 = R2 = 10 kΩ.

  1. Unloaded: U2 = 12 V · 10 kΩ20 kΩ = 6 V.
  2. Load RL = 10 kΩ: Rpar = 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ, so U2 = 12 V · 5 kΩ15 kΩ = 4 V.
  3. Load RL = 1 MΩ: Rpar = 10 · 10001010 kΩ ≈ 9.90 kΩ, so U2 ≈ 12 V · 9.9019.90 ≈ 5.97 V.

Check with the closed formula for RL = 10 kΩ: 10 · 1010 · 10 + 10 · 20 = 13, and 12 V · 13 = 4 V. A load a hundred times R2 costs only 0.5 %.

Current divider

Unloaded current dividerInItotal = GnGtotaltwo resistors: I1I2 = R2R1, I2I = R1R1 + R2Read it as: “each branch current is in the same ratio to the total as its conductance is to the total”.Only for parallel branches with one shared voltage: each branch takes a share of the current in proportion to its conductance. With two resistors the larger current flows through the smaller resistance, so the formula has the other resistor on top.
Loaded current dividerI2I = R1R1 + R2 + RLRead it as: “I two over I is R one over the sum of all three”.A load RL in series with R2 forms Rser = R2 + RL; then the two-resistor rule applies with Rser in place of R2.

Derivation: current divider

  1. One voltage across both branches: U = IG1 + G2, from KCL I = G1U + G2U.
  2. Branch current: I2 = G2U = G2G1 + G2 I = R1R1 + R2 I, multiplying top and bottom by R1R2.

Sources and loads: power and efficiency

Power from a real voltage sourcePtotal = U0I = U02Ri + RLPL = U2RL = U02 RL(Ri + RL)2Read it as: “the load power is U nought squared times R L over the square of the total resistance”.Ptotal is what the ideal source supplies, PL what reaches the load. PL → 0 both for an open circuit (RL → ∞, no current) and a short circuit (RL → 0, no voltage), so it has a maximum in between.
Maximum power transfer and efficiencyRL = Ri ⇒ PL,max = U024Riη = PLPtotal = RLRi + RLRead it as: “the most power reaches the load when R L equals R i, and then the efficiency is one half”.Setting dPLdRL = 0 gives RL = Ri. Derivation: the best load and its efficiency

  1. Current: I = U0Ri + RL, so PL = I2RL = U02 RL(Ri + RL)2.
  2. Quotient rule: dPLdRL = U02 (Ri + RL)2 − 2RL(Ri + RL)(Ri + RL)4 = U02 Ri − RL(Ri + RL)3.
  3. This is zero only for RL = Ri; it is positive below and negative above, so that is a maximum: PL,max = U02 Ri(2Ri)2 = U024Ri.
  4. Efficiency: the same I flows through Ri and RL, so η = I2RLI2(Ri + RL) = RLRi + RL, which is 12 at RL = Ri.

Worked example · matched versus efficient

3.6 Analysing More Extensive Networks

The shortcuts of §3.5 run out once a network has several sources or no clean series and parallel groups. Linearity gives one more shortcut, superposition, and node-voltage analysis gives a method that always works.

In plain words: in a network of resistors and sources every equation is linear, so effects simply add. Superposition uses this: switch on one source at a time, solve, and add the partial answers. For a systematic solution, node-voltage analysis picks one node as zero potential and takes the other nodes’ potentials as the unknowns; every branch current is then a potential difference times a conductance, and KCL at each node gives the equations. The Wheatstone bridge is a classic network solved this way and used to measure resistances.

Superposition

Superposition principleX = Xa + Xb + …voltage source off → short circuitcurrent source off → open circuitRead it as: “the total response is the sum of the responses to each source alone”.X is any branch current or voltage; Xa, Xb, … are the values with only one source active. Internal resistances of real sources stay in place. Powers do not superpose: P = RI2 is quadratic, so (Ia + Ib)2 ≠ Ia2 + Ib2.

Worked example · two sources and one resistor current

U0 = 10 V with R1 = 2 Ω in series feeds a node; R2 = 3 Ω runs from that node to the return wire, and an ideal current source I0 = 1 A pushes current into the same node. Find the current I2 down through R2.

  1. Only U0 on (current source open): R1 and R2 are in series, I2a = 10 V5 Ω = 2 A.
  2. Only I0 on (voltage source shorted): R1 and R2 are in parallel across the current source; the current divider gives I2b = 1 A · 2 Ω5 Ω = 0.4 A.
  3. Add: I2 = 2 A + 0.4 A = 2.4 A, so R2 takes 2.42 · 3 W = 17.28 W.
  4. Adding the partial powers instead would give 12 W + 0.48 W = 12.48 W, which is wrong by the cross term 2 · 2 A · 0.4 A · 3 Ω = 4.8 W.

Check with KCL at the node: the current in R1 is (10 V − 7.2 V) ÷ 2 Ω = 1.4 A, plus 1 A from the source, gives 2.4 A into R2, and 2.4 A · 3 Ω = 7.2 V.

How many unknowns

Each branch has one current and one voltage, but its current–voltage rule ties them together, so every branch carries only one unknown: for a resistor either U or I (the other follows from Ohm’s law); for an ideal voltage source the current; for an ideal current source the voltage. A network with Nb branches has at most Nb unknowns. Each method of this section keeps some of them as unknowns (node-voltage analysis keeps only node potentials) and produces one independent linear equation per unknown it keeps.

Node-voltage analysis

  1. Choose a reference node n0 with φ0 = 0.
  2. Number the other nodes n1, …, nNn − 1.
  3. Enclose nodes joined by an ideal voltage source: their potentials differ by the known source voltage, so the enclosure counts as one node.
  4. Give each node, or each enclosure, one unknown potential φ.
  5. Give every branch a current with a reference arrow.
  6. Write KCL for every node and enclosure, with each resistor current written as a potential difference times a conductance and each current-source current inserted as given. Drop one equation: it is linearly dependent on the rest.
  7. Solve the linear system (Gaussian elimination or Cramer’s rule, Appendix B.7) and compute the wanted currents and voltages.
Branch current from two potentialsIk = φx − φyRk = (φx − φy)GkRead it as: “the current from x to y is the potential difference times the conductance”.The arrow of the current points from node x to node y through the resistor (load convention). Arranged by unknowns, each node equation has the sum of the conductances at that node as the coefficient of its own potential and minus the conductance to each neighbour as the other coefficients. (That pattern follows directly from the notes’ worked equations; the notes do not state it as a rule.)

Worked example · two unknown potentials

U0 = 10 V, R1 = 2 Ω, R2 = 4 Ω, R3 = 2 Ω, R4 = 5 Ω and I0 = 0.5 A, connected as in the figure.

  1. Steps 1–4: n0 is the reference; the source encloses n0 and n1, so φ1 = 10 V; the unknowns are φ2 and φ3.
  2. KCL at n2 (inflow positive): 10 V − φ22 Ω − φ24 Ω − φ2 − φ32 Ω = 0, that is 1.25 S · φ2 − 0.5 S · φ3 = 5 A.
  3. KCL at n3: φ2 − φ32 Ω − φ35 Ω + 0.5 A = 0, that is −0.5 S · φ2 + 0.7 S · φ3 = 0.5 A.
  4. Cramer: the determinant is 1.25 · 0.7 − 0.25 = 0.625, so φ2 = 5 · 0.7 + 0.5 · 0.50.625 V = 6 V and φ3 = 1.25 · 0.5 + 0.5 · 50.625 V = 5 V.
  5. Currents: R1 carries 2 A, R2 1.5 A, R3 0.5 A and R4 1 A.

Check the power balance: the resistors take 8 + 9 + 0.5 + 5 = 22.5 W; the voltage source delivers 10 V · 2 A = 20 W and the current source 5 V · 0.5 A = 2.5 W, together 22.5 W.

The Wheatstone bridge

Diagonal voltage and balance conditionUD = GxG4 − G2G3(Gx + G2 + Gi)(G3 + G4 + Gi) − Gi2 U0UD = 0 ⇔ RxR4 = R2R3 ⇔ RxR2 = R3R4Read it as: “the bridge is balanced when R x times R four equals R two times R three”.The first line comes from node-voltage analysis with φ2 and φ3 unknown (derivation below). In practice one known resistor (here R2) is adjusted until the meter reads zero; then both sides are unloaded dividers with equal ratios. At balance no current flows through the meter, so its finite Ri does not falsify the result.

Derivation: the diagonal voltage

  1. Reference n0 at the bottom; the source fixes φ1 = U0; unknowns φ2 (left tap) and φ3 (right tap); the meter is a conductance Gi between the taps.
  2. KCL at n2: (U0 − φ2)Gx − φ2G2 − (φ2 − φ3)Gi = 0. At n3: (U0 − φ3)G3 − φ3G4 + (φ2 − φ3)Gi = 0.
  3. Sorted: (Gx + G2 + Gi)φ2 − Giφ3 = U0Gx and −Giφ2 + (G3 + G4 + Gi)φ3 = U0G3.
  4. Cramer’s rule gives φ2 and φ3 over the same determinant D; in UD = φ2 − φ3 the terms GxG3, GxGi and G3Gi cancel, leaving UD = GxG4 − G2G3D U0.
  5. UD = 0 exactly when GxG4 = G2G3, that is RxR4 = R2R3.

Worked example · finding an unknown resistance

A bridge has R3 = 470 Ω and R4 = 1 kΩ. The meter reads zero when R2 = 100 Ω.

  1. Balance: Rx = R2R3R4 = 100 Ω · 470 Ω1000 Ω = 47 Ω.
  2. Check the dividers with U0 = 10 V: left tap 10 V · 100147 ≈ 6.80 V, right tap 10 V · 10001470 ≈ 6.80 V.

Check: equal tap potentials, so UD = 0 and the meter current is zero, whatever its Ri.

Trap: switching off a voltage source means replacing it with a wire, not removing it. Removing it would leave an open circuit and change the network.

Sign check: in each node equation the coefficient of the node’s own potential is positive (the sum of its conductances) and every neighbour’s coefficient is negative. A sign that breaks this pattern points to a wrong current arrow.

Memory hook: superposition: voltage source off = short, current source off = open; add currents and voltages, never powers.

Near miss: in the superposition example, keeping the 10 V source while computing the 1 A part gives a “partial” current that already contains the first part, so the sum counts it twice. Every source except one must be off in each step.

Explain it

Answer aloud in complete sentences. Then compare with the model answer. (Retrieval and explanatory questioning [MIS, AMN, NEW].)

  1. Why may an ideal voltage source not be short-circuited, and an ideal current source not be left open?
  2. Why do the two equivalent real sources behave differently inside even though no load can tell them apart?
  3. Why does KVL hold for a mesh that is not a closed chain of wires?
  4. When can you use the voltage-divider formula?
  5. Why is maximum power transfer not what a power station wants?
  6. Why can superposition add currents but not powers?
  7. Why does node-voltage analysis drop one KCL equation?
  8. Why does the balanced Wheatstone bridge give a correct result with a non-ideal voltmeter?
Show model answers
  1. Each would need an unbounded quantity: infinite current through a zero-resistance short, or infinite voltage to drive a fixed current through an open gap. Real sources avoid this because their internal resistance limits the current or takes the current.
  2. Equivalence only means the same terminal line U = U0 − IRi. With open terminals the voltage source carries no current, while the current source sends all of I0 through its Ri and heats it; with a short circuit the roles swap.
  3. KVL comes from the potential: any closed path returns to its starting potential, so the potential differences along it add to zero whether or not a wire follows the path.
  4. When the same current flows through every resistor of the chain, so nothing draws current from a tap. With a load at the tap, first combine it in parallel with the lower resistor.
  5. At maximum power half of the source’s power is lost in its internal resistance. A power system needs high efficiency, so its loads are much larger than the internal resistance.
  6. Currents and voltages are linear in the source values, so partial solutions add. Power is quadratic in the current, so adding partial powers misses the cross terms.
  7. The node equations add up to zero over the whole network, because every branch current leaves one node and enters another. One equation is therefore a combination of the others and adds no information.
  8. At balance the two taps have the same potential, so no current flows through the meter. Its internal resistance then has no voltage across it and cannot change the circuit.

  4. Printed page 183, label: the figure calls the two-terminal network “one-pole”, while the text says one-port. One-port is meant.
  5. Printed page 233, superposition power: the line comparing partial powers ends with P2 − 2I2aI2b. The cross term must carry the resistance: P2 − 2I2aI2bR2, otherwise the units do not match.
  6. Printed pages 239 and 241, notation: the node count is written NK and the nodes K0K1, K2, K3 (from the German “Knoten”), where the rest of the chapter uses Nn and n0, n1, …. Not an error in the result.
  7. Printed page 248, Eq. (3.6.1): the numerator uses Gx for the unknown resistor, but the denominator still writes G1 for the same resistor. Both mean the conductance of Rx = R1.
